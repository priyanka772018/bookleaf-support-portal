# Approach — BookLeaf Author Support Portal

**What I prioritised.** The brief weights AI integration and architecture highest, and says to favour the core ticket flow over extras. So I first built the end-to-end loop — author raises ticket → AI triages → admin opens it and gets a grounded draft → edits and sends → author sees it live — and made every link of that loop survive the AI being unavailable. Then I spent the remaining time on what makes AI output trustworthy enough for a real ops team to send.

**How the AI is designed.** Two separate, small calls on Claude Haiku rather than one big one. *Triage* returns category and priority together via a forced tool with enum fields, using a rubric derived from BookLeaf policy (ISBN errors are always High; long-unpaid royalties are Critical) and no knowledge base. *Drafting* gets only the knowledge-base sections and sample-response guidance for the ticket's category, the last few messages, and — the most important piece — an **account facts sheet computed in code**: next payout deadline, whether pending money is below the ₹1,000 threshold, how long since the last payout, an explicit OVERDUE flag, and the production stage a book is in. LLMs are poor at date arithmetic and prone to inventing numbers; doing the maths deterministically is what lets drafts be specific ("due by 14 November", "₹850 rolls over") without being wrong. Drafts also return a short checklist of things the agent should verify, which is where the model puts anything the data doesn't contain instead of guessing.

**Trade-offs I made.**
- *Reliability over cleverness:* every ticket gets instant keyword triage before any AI call, so the queue is never empty or blocked; the AI result upgrades it seconds later. A circuit breaker stops a provider outage from turning into a timeout on every request. Fallbacks are labelled in the UI so agents know what to double-check.
- *Cost over completeness of context:* sectioned KB, truncated history and a per-conversation draft cache (re-opening a ticket costs zero tokens) keep a draft at ~1.2k input tokens regardless of thread length. Every call is logged so spend and failure rate are visible on an AI Usage page.
- *Simplicity over scale:* SSE with an in-process event bus, in-process background jobs and local-disk attachments are right for one instance and a 4-day build, and each sits behind a small interface (`publish/subscribe`, `triageInBackground`, `uploadPath`) so it can be swapped without touching the rest.
- *Humans stay in charge:* AI never sends anything; admin overrides of category/priority are never overwritten by later AI runs, and every override is recorded.

**Evolving this into production.**
1. Replace in-process pieces with durable infrastructure: a job queue (pg-boss/BullMQ) for triage with retries; Postgres LISTEN/NOTIFY or Redis for events across instances; S3 for attachments; httpOnly-cookie sessions.
2. Close the quality loop: measure how often admins override triage and how much they edit drafts (edit distance per category), turn real resolved tickets into an evaluation set, and tune prompts against it before every change. Add retrieval over past resolved tickets as few-shot examples once there is history.
3. Connect real systems: per-platform royalty statements, bank-link status and production-stage timestamps from the source systems, so drafts can quote exact figures and the checklist shrinks.
4. Operational features: email notifications, SLA reporting, canned macros, and routing by category to specialist teams (finance for royalties, production for ISBN).
